\begin{abstract}
Recursive training can amplify errors and erode low-probability behavior when later models learn from earlier models' outputs, yet prior work also shows that accumulation, real-data mixing, verification, and careful selection can stabilize particular workflows. We study a narrower resource-allocation question: given a recursive horizon and one fixed lifetime budget of human-origin tokens actually consumed by the optimizer, does allocating that budget jointly across generation and monitored human-distribution mode improve chain-level retention relative to matched non-joint policies? The planned experiment compares four policy families---fresh random rescue, schedule-only allocation, selection-only allocation, and joint time-and-mode allocation---while matching both lifetime human-token exposure and total optimizer-token exposure. One independently seeded recursive chain is the experimental unit. Planned primary outcomes are area under the held-out human negative-log-likelihood-regret curve and a separately frozen tail-retention measure; an incomplete-monitor intervention tests whether targeted allocation can optimize a biased reference while damaging unobserved modes. The scope is one licensed text domain, one screening-scale language model, a frozen positive control, five disjoint data partitions, and paired chains under preregistered validity rules. \textbf{RESULT\_PENDING:} no novel treatment outcome has been inspected or is asserted. The manuscript will support a favorable, equivalent, harmful, or inconclusive conclusion, and the provisional novelty claim is limited to the conjunction of recursion, exact lifetime human-token accounting, joint time-and-mode allocation, and matched schedule-only and selection-only baselines.
\end{abstract}
